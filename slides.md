# Active Voice

<div class="lucide-icon-block">
<span class="i-lucide-arrow-right text-4xl text-blue-500"></span>
</div>

The **subject performs** the action expressed in the verb.

> The dog bit the boy.

Subject (*the dog*) → acts → object (*the boy*)

::right::

<div class="mt-16">

### Why it usually wins
- Clear meaning
- Direct
- Concise — fewer words needed

</div>

<!--
Purdue OWL example: "the dog bit the boy" — arrow points from subject
performing the action to the individual being acted upon. Active voice is the
default for most non-scientific writing.
-->

---
layout: two-cols
---

# Passive Voice

<div class="lucide-icon-block">
<span class="i-lucide-arrow-left-right text-4xl text-rose-500"></span>
</div>

The **subject receives** the action; it's acted upon.

> The boy was bitten by the dog.

Subject (*the boy*) ← receives ← agent (*the dog*)

::right::

<div class="mt-16">

### How to spot it
- A form of **be**: is, am, was, were, been, are
- Often a **"by the..."** phrase naming the agent
- The agent may be omitted entirely

</div>

<!--
Key teaching point for later: a be-verb alone does NOT guarantee passive voice.
"She was happy" is not passive — there's no action being received. Save this
nuance for the "common pitfall" slide.
-->

# Side-by-Side

<div class="grid grid-cols-2 gap-6 mt-6">

<div class="p-4 rounded-lg bg-blue-50">
<span class="i-lucide-check-circle text-blue-500 text-2xl"></span>

### Active
**Scientists conducted the experiment.**

Subject (*scientists*) performs the action.
Concise: 4 words to the verb phrase.
</div>

<div class="p-4 rounded-lg bg-rose-50">
<span class="i-lucide-circle text-rose-500 text-2xl"></span>

### Passive
**The experiment was conducted by scientists.**

Subject (*experiment*) receives the action.
Same idea, more words, agent pushed to the end.
</div>

</div>

# When Passive Voice Is the *Better* Choice

<div class="grid grid-cols-2 gap-4 mt-6">
<div class="p-4 border rounded-lg">
<span class="i-lucide-user-x text-2xl text-gray-600"></span>

**Agent is unknown**
"The window was broken sometime last night."
</div>
<div class="p-4 border rounded-lg">
<span class="i-lucide-shield text-2xl text-gray-600"></span>

**Agent is obvious or unimportant**
"The suspect was arrested at noon."
</div>
<div class="p-4 border rounded-lg">
<span class="i-lucide-clock text-2xl text-gray-600"></span>

**Writer wants to delay/downplay the agent**
Used for tact, diplomacy, or emphasis
</div>
<div class="p-4 border rounded-lg">
<span class="i-lucide-flask-conical text-2xl text-gray-600"></span>

**Scientific & academic writing**
Keeps focus on the action/data, not the researcher
</div>
</div>

<!--
Purdue OWL: passive makes sense when the agent is obvious, unimportant, or
unknown, or when a writer wants to postpone or avoid naming the agent. It
highlights the action and the receiver rather than the doer.
-->

# Passive Voice & the "Objective" Voice of Science

<span class="i-lucide-microscope text-3xl text-emerald-600"></span>

> "The samples were heated to 100°C and observed for changes in color."

- No personal pronouns, no named researcher
- Creates the *appearance* of objective, fact-based reporting
- Keeps the focus on the **method and results**, not the person doing them

<div class="mt-6 p-3 bg-amber-50 rounded-lg text-sm">
⚠️ Caution: overusing passive voice — even in science writing — can make prose
flat and obscure meaning. It's a rhetorical tool, not a mandate.
</div>

<!--
This is a great bridge to research writing and MLA source use — passive voice
shows up constantly in the scientific/academic sources students are citing.
-->

# Converting Passive → Active

1. Find the agent (often in a "by the..." phrase)
2. If no agent is stated, infer it from context
3. Make the agent the **subject**
4. Adjust the verb accordingly

<div class="mt-4 p-3 bg-blue-50 rounded-lg text-sm">
<strong>Before:</strong> The proposal was rejected by the committee.<br/>
<strong>After:</strong> The committee rejected the proposal.
</div>

::right::

# Converting Active → Passive

1. Identify who/what performs the action
2. Turn that agent into a "by the..." phrase (or drop it)
3. Make the receiver of the action the **subject**
4. Shift the verb to *be* + past participle

<div class="mt-4 p-3 bg-rose-50 rounded-lg text-sm">
<strong>Before:</strong> The committee rejected the proposal.<br/>
<strong>After:</strong> The proposal was rejected by the committee.
</div>

<!--
Have students practice both directions live — converting in only one
direction reinforces the misconception that active is "correct" and passive
is a mistake. It's a choice, not an error.
-->

# Common Pitfall

<span class="i-lucide-alert-triangle text-3xl text-amber-500"></span>

### A "be" verb does not automatically mean passive voice.

<div class="grid grid-cols-2 gap-6 mt-6">
<div class="p-4 rounded-lg bg-gray-50">

**Not passive:**
"She **was** tired after the race."

*Was* here is a linking verb — no action is being received.
</div>
<div class="p-4 rounded-lg bg-rose-50">

**Passive:**
"The race **was** finished by every runner."

*Was finished* is be + past participle — an action being received.
</div>
</div>

<!--
This is the single most common student error when hunting for passive voice
in their own drafts — they flag every "was/is/were" and miss the ones that
are actually just linking verbs.
</div>
-->

# From Grammar to Rhetoric

<span class="i-lucide-target text-3xl text-indigo-600"></span>

Spotting active/passive voice is DOK 1–2. The real work is asking:

<div class="text-xl mt-6 space-y-3">

- **Why** did this writer choose this voice *here*?
- What does it emphasize — or hide?
- Would the sentence work *better* in the other voice? Why or why not?

</div>
